# CV Evaluation Criteria for Recruiters

## Metadata
- **Topic**: Candidate CV / Resume Evaluation
- **Audience**: HR Professionals, Recruiters
- **Language**: English
- **Version**: 2.0 (Expanded Edition)

---

## 1. Professional Qualifications, Education & Relevant Skills

**Description**: The foundational criterion that determines whether a candidate meets the basic requirements of the role.

### What to Evaluate
- Degree or field of study relevant to the applied position
- Hard skills and tools directly mentioned in the Job Description (JD)
- Supporting certifications or courses demonstrating up-to-date knowledge

### Examples by Role

| Role | Key Skills / Certifications to Look For |
|------|-----------------------------------------|
| Data Analyst | Advanced Excel, SQL, Power BI, Python, data analytics or statistics certifications |
| Accounting Specialist | Bachelor's in Accounting/Finance, ACCA/CPA certification, proficiency in MISA/SAP |

### HR Tip
> Don't just read the degree on paper. Assess how closely the candidate's skills align with the JD — that reflects real-world applicability, not just theoretical knowledge.

## 2. Work Experience Aligned with the JD

**Description**: The "backbone" of CV evaluation. Helps HR quickly gauge how well a candidate's actual capabilities match the role's requirements.

### What to Evaluate

#### 2.1 Tenure and Stability
- Frequent job-hopping (under 6 months per company) may indicate lack of commitment
- Consider industry context — agencies and startups naturally have higher turnover

#### 2.2 Relevance to the Open Role
- Check for similar responsibilities, tools, workflows, and KPIs to the current role

#### 2.3 Concrete Achievements (with numbers)
- "Grew revenue by 25%"
- "Reduced advertising costs by 15%"
- "Led a team of 10 to complete Project A within 3 months"

### Examples by Role

| Role | Key Experience Signals |
|------|------------------------|
| Digital Marketing Executive | Multi-channel campaign execution, customer insight analysis, improved conversion rate, reduced CPC |
| B2B Sales | Sales results, client portfolio size, industry segments handled |

### HR Tip
> Look for keywords in the experience section that match the JD — these are strong indicators of fit. Also verify the timeline logic to catch unexplained employment gaps or inflated experience claims.

---

## 3. Notable Achievements & Projects

**Description**: This section separates candidates who "did the work" from those who "did it well" — proving real impact, not just listing responsibilities.

### What to Evaluate

#### 3.1 Quantifiable Results
- "Grew sales by 30% in Q2 2024"
- "Reduced operational costs by 20% through process optimization"
- "Launched a marketing campaign reaching 5 million impressions"

#### 3.2 Relevance to the Applied Role
- Focus on projects that demonstrate skills and thinking aligned with the target position
- Example: For a Project Manager role → look for achievements in timeline management, cross-team coordination, and on-budget delivery

#### 3.3 Candidate's Actual Role in the Project
- Distinguish between lead contributor vs. support role
- A strong CV should clarify: "Directly developed content, managed a $8,700 budget, and reported results to the department head"

### HR Tip
> Prioritize data-driven CVs. Metrics such as KPI, ROI, conversion rate, revenue, and traffic are stronger evidence than any descriptive statement. Compare the scale of the project to the scope of the open role to assess real compatibility.

## 4. Cultural Fit & Career Development Alignment

**Description**: A key factor in long-term retention. A high-performing candidate who clashes with company culture can reduce team morale and overall performance.

### What to Evaluate

#### 4.1 Tone and Presentation Style of the CV

| CV Style | Best Fit Environment |
|----------|----------------------|
| Professional, concise, structured thinking | Corporate environments with defined processes and discipline |
| Creative, open, flexible language | Dynamic environments like agencies or startups |

#### 4.2 Extracurricular Activities, Interests & Personal Projects
- Volunteering, mentoring, leading clubs → outgoing personality, leadership mindset, high sense of responsibility

#### 4.3 Career Direction (Objective / Summary Section)
- Check whether the candidate's stated goals align with the company's growth path
- Example: A candidate aiming to "become a Team Leader within 2 years" is a strong match if the company plans to develop managerial talent

### HR Tip
> Read the CV asking: **"Can this person thrive and grow here?"** — not just "Can they do the job?" Cultural fit reduces early attrition and drives long-term engagement.

---

## 5. Consistency, Clarity & Professional Presentation

**Description**: How a CV is presented reflects the candidate's professionalism, logical thinking, and seriousness about the role.

### What to Evaluate

#### 5.1 Content Consistency
- No overlapping employment dates
- Job descriptions match job titles
- Content aligns with public profiles (e.g., LinkedIn)

#### 5.2 Clarity and Information Structure
A well-structured CV should include:
1. Personal information
2. Career objective
3. Work experience
4. Skills
5. Achievements
6. Education

Formatting requirements: logical layout, bullet points, consistent formatting, appropriate spacing.

#### 5.3 Professional Presentation
- Free of spelling and grammar errors
- Consistent font, color scheme, and layout
- Professional contact email address

### HR Tip
> A candidate who is meticulous about their CV is often meticulous in their work. A messy or careless resume is an early warning sign worth noting.

## 6. Identifying Red Flags vs. Yellow Flags

**Description**: Not all CV anomalies are instant disqualifiers. HR professionals must distinguish between hard "Red Flags" (reject) and "Yellow Flags" (probe during the interview).

### 6.1 Red Flags (High Risk — Often leads to rejection)
- **Unprofessional email addresses** (e.g., `badboy_99@email.com`)
- **Toxic language**: Blaming or speaking negatively about past employers in the summary or experience sections
- **Plagiarism or extreme exaggeration**: Claiming sole credit for massive enterprise-level projects that typically require an entire team
- **Blatant inconsistencies**: Dates on the CV do not match the candidate's LinkedIn profile

### 6.2 Yellow Flags (Needs Context — Probe in screening call)
- **Employment gaps**: A 6-month gap could be due to a layoff, health reasons, or a sabbatical. It requires a question, not an instant rejection
- **Short tenures (Job-hopping)**: If a candidate has three 8-month stints, it's a yellow flag. However, if they were contract roles, agency work, or due to startup bankruptcies, it is justified
- **Career pivots**: A sudden shift in industry (e.g., Sales to Software Engineering) might look disjointed but could demonstrate high adaptability if supported by a portfolio

### HR Tip
> Always give the benefit of the doubt to Yellow Flags if the candidate's core skills strongly match the JD. Use the initial phone screen to ask directly: "Can you walk me through the transition between Company A and Company B?"

## 7. Assessing Soft Skills "Between the Lines"

**Description**: Any candidate can write "Excellent Communication" or "Detail-Oriented" under their skills section. Strong recruiters evaluate soft skills based on *how* the CV is written, rather than what is explicitly claimed.

### How to Evaluate Hidden Soft Skills

| Claimed Soft Skill | How to Verify It Through the CV |
|--------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Communication** | Is the CV concise, highly readable, and free of fluff? Can they explain complex technical tasks in simple business terms? |
| **Attention to Detail** | Are there zero spelling/grammar errors? Is the formatting (fonts, bullet points, margins) 100% consistent throughout? |
| **Problem Solving** | Do their bullet points follow a logical flow (e.g., Problem → Action → Result)? Do they mention overcoming specific bottlenecks? |
| **User-Centricity** | (For Design/Product roles) Is the CV itself well-designed with a clear visual hierarchy and good typography? |

### HR Tip
> A CV is the candidate's first practical test in written communication and empathy for the reader (the recruiter). If a candidate claims to be "Detail-Oriented" but has mismatched bullet points and typos, the evidence contradicts the claim.

## 8. Evaluating AI-Assisted CVs (The ChatGPT Era)

**Description**: With the rise of Generative AI, many candidates use LLMs to write or polish their CVs. Recruiters must know how to distinguish between a thoughtfully AI-assisted CV and a lazily AI-generated one.

### 8.1 Signs of Heavy, Unedited AI Usage
- **Overly complex, unnatural vocabulary**: Words like "Spearheaded a paradigm shift," "Delved into," "Navigated complex multifaceted landscapes"
- **Lack of specifics**: AI hallucinates structure but lacks personal data. Look out for bullet points that sound perfect but contain no actual metrics, tool names, or specific project contexts
- **Generic Summaries**: A highly generic objective statement that could apply to any candidate in the industry

### 8.2 How to Handle AI CVs
- **Do not penalize formatting/grammar assistance**: Using AI to fix grammar or structure is a smart use of tools
- **Demand authenticity in the interview**: If the CV reads like an AI generated it, focus the interview strictly on technical depth and behavioral questions (STAR method) to verify if the candidate actually possesses the depth of knowledge implied in the text

### HR Tip
> A strong candidate uses AI as a structural foundation but fills it with personal data, exact metrics, and niche technical challenges. A weak candidate copies and pastes the prompt output directly. Look for the "human data" inside the perfect grammar.

## 9. Role-Specific Evaluation Frameworks

**Description**: Generic evaluation criteria are a starting point, but strong recruiters apply role-specific lenses. The signals that indicate a great Data Analyst CV are fundamentally different from those of a great UX Designer CV. This section provides targeted evaluation frameworks for the most commonly recruited role families.

### 9.1 Technical & Engineering Roles

*Applicable to: Software Engineers, Data Engineers, DevOps, QA Engineers, Data Analysts, Data Scientists*

| Signal | What to Look For | Yellow Flag |
|--------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Tech stack specificity** | Exact tool and language versions (e.g., "Python 3.11, FastAPI, PostgreSQL 15") rather than vague claims like "proficient in databases" | Lists 15+ technologies with no depth on any |
| **System scale indicators** | Mentions of data volume, traffic load, or user base (e.g., "handled 2M daily API requests", "processed 10TB monthly") | All projects described at a small or unspecified scale |
| **Ownership signals** | "Architected", "Designed from scratch", "Led migration from X to Y" vs. "Assisted with", "Supported team in" | All bullets use passive or supporting-role language |
| **Version control & collaboration** | Mentions of GitHub, GitLab, code review practices, CI/CD pipelines | No mention of collaborative development workflows |
| **Problem-outcome pairing** | Each technical achievement paired with a business result (e.g., "Reduced query execution time by 60%, cutting monthly cloud costs by $3,200") | Technical achievements listed with no business context |

**Red Flag specific to technical roles:** A candidate claims proficiency in a technology released after their last employment date — suggests keyword padding rather than genuine experience.

### 9.2 Marketing & Growth Roles

*Applicable to: Digital Marketing, Content, SEO/SEM, Performance Marketing, Brand, Product Marketing*

| Signal | What to Look For | Yellow Flag |
|--------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Channel specificity** | Named platforms and tools (Google Ads, Meta Business Manager, HubSpot, SEMrush, Klaviyo) with budget or scale context | Generic "managed social media" with no platform names |
| **Funnel-stage thinking** | Demonstrates understanding across TOFU/MOFU/BOFU (awareness, lead gen, conversion, retention) | Only mentions one stage of the funnel |
| **Performance metrics ownership** | "Owned CAC, ROAS, and LTV targets for a $120K/month paid media budget" | Lists metrics passively without ownership language |
| **Test-and-learn mindset** | References A/B testing, experimentation, or data-driven iteration | No mention of testing or optimization methodologies |
| **Cross-functional collaboration** | Worked with product, sales, or engineering teams to deliver marketing outcomes | CV reads as purely isolated marketing execution |

### 9.3 Sales & Business Development Roles

*Applicable to: B2B Sales, Account Executive, Business Development, Key Account Manager, Sales Manager*

| Signal | What to Look For | Yellow Flag |
|--------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Quota attainment** | Explicit percentage of quota met ("Achieved 118% of annual quota") or ranking ("Top 3 out of 24-person sales team") | Revenue numbers cited without quota context |
| **Deal size and cycle length** | Context on average contract value (ACV) and sales cycle (e.g., "ACV $45K, 60-day average sales cycle") | No indication of deal complexity or size |
| **Pipeline ownership** | Demonstrates prospecting, qualification, and closing (full-cycle) vs. only inbound or only closing | Experience appears limited to one stage of the sales cycle |
| **Industry vertical familiarity** | Named industries, buyer personas, or decision-maker types (e.g., "Sold to VP Engineering and CTO in SaaS and fintech") | Generic "B2B sales" with no vertical or buyer specificity |
| **CRM usage** | Salesforce, HubSpot CRM, Pipedrive — with usage context (forecasting, pipeline hygiene) | No CRM mentioned at all for a role requiring one |

### 9.4 Design & Creative Roles

*Applicable to: UI/UX Designer, Graphic Designer, Product Designer, Motion Designer, Creative Director*

| Signal | What to Look For | Yellow Flag |
|--------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Process documentation** | Mentions of user research, wireframing, prototyping, usability testing — not just "designed screens" | CV describes only final deliverables with no process context |
| **Tool specificity** | Figma, Adobe XD, Sketch, After Effects, Premiere Pro — with specific project context | "Proficient in Adobe Suite" without specifying which tools or how |
| **Portfolio link quality** | A clean, working URL to a personal portfolio site or Behance/Dribbble — not just a generic LinkedIn | Portfolio link is broken, behind a password, or absent entirely |
| **Business impact of design** | "Redesigned onboarding flow, reducing drop-off rate by 34%" — design connected to measurable outcomes | Design described purely aesthetically with no user or business impact |
| **Collaboration with product/engineering** | Design handoff process, working with developers in Figma Dev Mode, or involvement in sprint cycles | No mention of how designs were implemented or handed off |

### 9.5 Operations & Project Management Roles

*Applicable to: Operations Manager, Project Manager, Business Analyst, Supply Chain, Process Improvement*

| Signal | What to Look For | Yellow Flag |
|--------|-----------------|-------------|
| **Methodology fluency** | Agile, Scrum, Kanban, Six Sigma, PRINCE2 — with evidence of real application, not just listed | Certifications listed with no examples of how the methodology was applied |
| **Stakeholder management evidence** | "Coordinated across 5 departments", "Presented to C-suite quarterly" — named stakeholder types | All experience described at team level only, no cross-functional exposure |
| **Delivery track record** | Projects delivered on time, under budget, or with documented scope management | No mention of deadlines, budgets, or delivery outcomes |
| **Process improvement metrics** | "Reduced cycle time by 35%", "Cut error rate from 8% to 1.2% through SOP restructuring" | Process work described in terms of activities only, not outcomes |
| **Tools and platforms** | Jira, Asana, Monday.com, MS Project, Notion, Tableau — matched to the role's context | Generic "MS Office proficiency" for a modern operations role |

## 10. Scoring CVs Consistently: Building an Evaluation Rubric

**Description**: Subjective CV evaluation introduces bias and inconsistency across a hiring team. Standardizing the process with a structured scoring rubric improves both fairness and decision quality.

### 10.1 Why Rubrics Matter
- Multiple recruiters reviewing the same CV pool will apply different standards without a rubric
- Rubrics create a defensible, documented basis for shortlisting decisions
- Structured scoring reduces the risk of affinity bias (favoring candidates from similar backgrounds)

### 10.2 Sample Scoring Rubric (Adaptable by Role)

Score each criterion on a 1–4 scale:

| Score | Descriptor |
|-------|-----------|
| 4 — Exceeds | Fully meets criteria and demonstrates additional depth, scale, or leadership |
| 3 — Meets | Clearly meets criteria with specific, verifiable evidence |
| 2 — Partial | Partially meets criteria; evidence is vague or incomplete |
| 1 — Does Not Meet | Does not meet criteria; significant gaps relative to the JD |

| Criterion | Weight | Score (1–4) | Weighted Score |
|-----------|--------|-------------|----------------|
| Education & qualifications | 10% | | |
| Relevant work experience | 25% | | |
| Key skills / tools match | 20% | | |
| Measurable achievements | 20% | | |
| Cultural / career fit signals | 15% | | |
| Presentation & consistency | 10% | | |
| **Total** | **100%** | | **/4.0** |

### 10.3 Using the Rubric in Practice

**Step 1 — Calibration session:** Before reviewing a new batch of CVs, the hiring team reviews two or three sample CVs together and agrees on scores. This aligns the team on what a "3" vs. a "4" looks like for that specific role.

**Step 2 — Independent scoring:** Each recruiter or hiring manager scores independently to avoid anchoring bias (where the first reviewer's opinion shapes everyone else's).

**Step 3 — Debrief on outliers:** Any CV where two reviewers' total scores differ by more than 0.5 points should be discussed before a shortlisting decision is made.

**Step 4 — Set a shortlist threshold:** Define the minimum weighted score for advancing to a phone screen (e.g., 2.8/4.0 or higher). This threshold can be adjusted based on the size of the applicant pool.

### 10.4 Bias Awareness in CV Evaluation

Even with a rubric, biases can influence scoring. HR professionals should actively watch for:

| Bias Type | Description | Mitigation |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| **Affinity bias** | Favoring candidates from the same university, city, or background as the reviewer | Use blind screening (remove name/photo/address) for the first round |
| **Halo effect** | One strong signal (e.g., a prestigious employer) inflates all other scores | Score each criterion independently before forming an overall impression |
| **Recency bias** | Over-weighting the most recent role, ignoring earlier relevant experience | Read the full CV before scoring any criterion |
| **Name-based bias** | Unconsciously rating candidates differently based on perceived ethnicity from their name | Implement anonymized CV review for shortlisting in diverse hiring initiatives |

## 11. Verification & Background Screening Basics

**Description**: CV claims are self-reported. Strong recruiters have a systematic approach to verifying key information before extending an offer — and ideally before investing significant interview time.

### 11.1 What to Verify and When

| Claim Type | Verification Method | Recommended Stage |
|------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Employment dates and titles | Reference check or LinkedIn cross-reference | Before final interview |
| Academic qualifications | Degree certificate request or institutional verification | Before offer |
| Professional certifications | Direct check on issuing body's verification portal (e.g., PMI, Google, ACCA) | Before offer |
| Reported metrics (revenue, team size) | Reference check with direct manager or team lead | Before final interview |
| Portfolio / code samples | Live review or technical test | During interview process |

### 11.2 Certification Verification Resources

| Certification Body | Verification Method |
|--------------------|---------------------|
| PMP (PMI) | PMI credential verification portal: `credly.com` or `pmi.org/certifications/verify` |
| ACCA | ACCA global verification service: `acca.global` |
| Google Certifications | Credly badge link provided on the certificate |
| AWS Certifications | AWS certification verification: `aws.amazon.com/verification` |
| CFA | CFA Institute verification: `cfainstitute.org` |

### 11.3 Red Flag Patterns That Warrant Deeper Verification

- Claimed employment at a company that no longer exists (dissolved startup) — verify via LinkedIn company history or Companies House (UK) / business registry
- Metrics that seem statistically implausible (e.g., "Grew revenue from $50K to $50M in 8 months as a junior executive")
- Certifications listed without an issue date or with dates that predate the certification program's launch year
- Identical bullet points appearing on multiple candidates' CVs for the same role — may indicate a CV mill or shared template with fabricated data

### 11.4 Reference Check Best Practices

A reference check is not a formality — it is a structured data-gathering conversation. Use these questions:

1. "In what capacity did you work with [candidate], and for how long?"
2. "What were their primary responsibilities in your team?"
3. "Can you confirm the approximate size of the team they managed / budget they oversaw?"
4. "How did they handle pressure or tight deadlines? Can you give a specific example?"
5. "Is there any context I should know about the reason they left the role?"
6. "Would you rehire this person if the opportunity arose?"

> **HR Tip:** The most revealing reference check question is the last one. A hesitation, a qualified "yes," or a redirect to "HR policy" on rehirability is itself a data point worth probing.

## 12. Evaluation Workflow: From CV Receipt to Shortlist

**Description**: A structured end-to-end workflow ensures consistency, speed, and fairness across the entire CV review process — especially when managing high-volume hiring.

### 12.1 Recommended CV Review Workflow

```
Step 1: Initial Triage (< 60 seconds per CV)
         ↓
         Hard filter: Does the CV meet the non-negotiable requirements?
         (e.g., required degree, minimum years of experience, work authorization)
         → No  → Archive (with reason logged in ATS)
         → Yes → Proceed to Step 2

Step 2: Structured Scoring (5–10 minutes per CV)
         ↓
         Apply the evaluation rubric (Section 10)
         Score each criterion independently
         → Score < threshold → Hold pool (revisit if shortlist is thin)
         → Score ≥ threshold → Proceed to Step 3

Step 3: Red Flag & Yellow Flag Check (2–3 minutes per CV)
         ↓
         Check for Red Flags → If present, archive with reason logged
         Check for Yellow Flags → If present, note for screening call agenda

Step 4: Shortlist Compilation
         ↓
         Rank shortlisted candidates by weighted rubric score
         Debrief with hiring manager on top 5–8 candidates
         Agree on phone screen invitations

Step 5: Phone Screen Scheduling
         ↓
         Send standardized screening invitation
         Attach Yellow Flag notes to the interviewer's prep document
         Set a consistent SLA: screen within 5 business days of shortlisting
```

### 12.2 SLA Benchmarks for CV Review

| Hiring Stage | Recommended SLA |
|---|---|
| Initial triage after application | Within 3 business days |
| Full structured scoring | Within 5 business days of application close |
| Shortlist communicated to hiring manager | Within 7 business days |
| Phone screen scheduled | Within 10 business days of application |
| Offer stage (from final interview) | Within 5 business days |

> **Why SLAs matter:** Top candidates are often in multiple processes simultaneously. A slow review workflow — even with a strong shortlist — results in losing qualified candidates to faster-moving competitors.

### 12.3 High-Volume Hiring Adaptations

When reviewing 200+ CVs for a single role, the full rubric process is not always feasible for every application. Recommended adaptations:

- **Two-stage triage:** Apply hard filters in Round 1 (ATS keyword screen or a 30-second manual check on non-negotiables). Apply the full rubric only to CVs that pass Round 1.
- **Panel scoring:** Assign different CV batches to different team members to distribute load. Run a calibration session first to align scoring standards.
- **Standardized rejection language:** Maintain a bank of polite, specific rejection templates segmented by reason (underqualified, overqualified, role filled, location mismatch) to respond to every applicant — not just shortlisted ones.
